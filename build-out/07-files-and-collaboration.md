# 07 · Files and collaboration — where state lives, who can see it

> 🔨 hands-on — SaaS administration and the sharing/permission surface
> **Before:** 03 identity · 05 network · 06 tenant. **After:** 09 backup · 14 compliance evidence

The technical part of this step is small. The part that decides whether the next
three years are pleasant is **a naming and permission model that people will
actually follow**, and that is not a technical artefact.

The reason it sits after 03 is the same reason everything does: sharing is
expressed in groups, and groups were modelled in 03. If groups mean two things
there, they mean two things here — and here it is visible to everyone, every day.

## What this step produces

- A decision about **where a document lives by default**: personal drive, team space,
  or a records system. Ambiguity here is what fills personal drives with company
  records that leave when the person does.
- A permission model expressed **entirely in groups** — never individuals. One
  exception on day one becomes the norm by month six.
- An external sharing policy that has been decided rather than inherited, and that
  the SOC 2 customer can be told about in one sentence.
- A small number of named spaces, created deliberately, with owners.

## Questions to ask first

- **What is the default location for a new document, and can someone state it in one
  sentence?** If not, the answer in practice is "personal drive", and you have
  chosen the worst option by not choosing.
- **Who owns a space when its creator leaves?** Answer structurally, with a group,
  not by naming a person who will also eventually leave.
- **Can anything be shared to anyone, or does external sharing have a boundary?**
  Both are defensible; only one of them can be described to an auditor.
- **What must never be in here** — customer data, secrets, HR records? Say it now, and
  say where those go instead. "Don't put secrets in the wiki" without a secrets
  manager is a wish.
- **Does anyone check?** A permission model with no periodic review is a permission
  model as of the day it was written.
- **What does step 09 have to be able to restore, and from what?** Retention inside a
  suite is not backup; decide there, but scope it here.

## 2015 → today

| | 2015 | today |
|---|---|---|
| Where files live | a file server, with NTFS permissions and mapped drives | a SaaS suite, with sharing links |
| The hard problem | capacity, backup, and the share that filled up on Friday | **who can see what**, and proving it |
| Sharing outside | FTP, or an attachment | a link, generated by anyone, in one click |
| Leaver risk | files were on a server you controlled | files may be in a personal drive that deletes with the account |
| Audit question | "is it backed up?" | "who has access, and how do you know?" |

**How much of that is AI: none for the build, and a real one for the review.**
The move from file server to suite is SaaS-ification. But *"who can see what,
across a hundred spaces and thousands of links"* is genuinely hard to answer by
hand, and it is a good fit for automated summarising and anomaly-spotting —
surfacing the space shared to the whole internet, the link with no expiry, the
account with access to everything.

Same line as everywhere in this series: **let it find them; do not let it revoke
them.** An automated revocation that is wrong looks exactly like an outage, and the
person it hits is a colleague who was doing their job.

## Getting it backwards

**Permissions granted to individuals.** It is faster, it is one click, and it is
correct exactly once. Two years on, access is a graph nobody can read, an access
review is archaeology, and the honest answer to the auditor's question is "we would
have to look".

**No default location.** Everything lands in personal drives. Then someone leaves,
their account is deleted per policy, and a project's history goes with it. This is
usually discovered months later by someone looking for a contract.

**Deciding external sharing by not deciding.** The default in most suites is
permissive. Nobody chose it, everybody relies on it, and tightening it later breaks
live customer workflows on a random Tuesday.
